Technology and Values: The Core Framework

The Information Age is a modern era defined by access: many new technologies emerged in a short time, and billions of people got near-instant access to information.

Two catalysts of the Information Age
  1. Low-cost computers: computing power became affordable and personal.
  2. High-speed communication networks: information can move almost instantly.
The smartphone is the emblematic product: it combines both catalysts in one device.

A two-way street (the lens for the whole course)

Technology shapes usWe shape technology (and its side effects)
People invent and adopt technology, and adopted technology changes society.
It can change people physically: London taxi drivers' brains change after memorizing the streets.
It can have psychological effects, e.g. dependency on a phone.		 Every technology solved a real problem, but solving one problem often creates a new one:
• Automobile: mobility, but pollution and sprawl
• Refrigerator: food safety, but new energy demands
• Cheap international calls: connection, but privacy risks

Both directions happen at once. This reciprocal relationship between technology and human values is the lens for the whole course.

Case: The Amish and selective adoption
What happens
Many Amish communities neither accept nor reject technology as a whole. They test each new technology against their values before deciding whether, or how, to adopt it.
Result
Not "no technology" but deliberate technology, adopted only when it supports community, family and faith rather than eroding them.
Lesson
Adopting a technology can be an ethical choice, not something inevitable.

Social conditions create markets

The Gilded Age (rapid industrialization, economic growth, power concentrated in fewer and larger corporations) created a need for up-to-date, comprehensive, reliable and affordable information across many management layers and locations.

Key idea: technology rarely appears from nowhere. Social and economic conditions create the demand that calls it into being.

Calculators reshaped who did the work

  • Calculators were about 6× faster than adding by hand, so people of average ability became highly productive. This is called "deskilling".
  • As the skill needed dropped, wages dropped, and women were widely hired to replace men in bookkeeping (the "feminization of bookkeeping", mentioned again in Week 5).

Case: IBM and the Holocaust
What happened
Hitler came to power in Germany in 1933. IBM CEO Watson ignored anti-Semitic violence and the creation of concentration camps. IBM expanded its German subsidiary and sought contracts with the German government.
Use of the technology
The Nazis used IBM machines to run censuses and generate lists of Jews. The lists made it easier to seize assets and deport people to camps.
Issue
Who is responsible: the corporation, the engineers, or the sales staff? Could leaders have foreseen the use? What does this demand of people who build data systems today?
Lesson
Technology decisions, especially about data systems, carry real human consequences.

Information Technology Issues

Every new capability created a matching risk. No technology is inherently neutral in its effects, even when its design is neutral.

  • Positive: convenience, access and connection on a scale no earlier generation had.
  • Negative: computer-assisted crime, surveillance, and harms no law had covered before.
  • The scope of IT issues is wider than "technical": social, legal, ethical, economic, environmental.
Case: The first computer virus
What happened
A graduate student at the University of Southern California wrote five small programs on a VAX 11/750 running Unix. Within an hour, each one took full control of the system. He called them "viruses", the first documented use of the term for unwanted code.
Issue
It was framed as research. Does framing change the ethics?
Lesson
A single individual (not a nation or company) could compromise a shared system. The capability did not depend on intent.

Issue spotlight: ownership, privacy and a connected world

  • Piracy & ownership: digitization makes perfect copies and the Internet spreads them instantly and cheaply (one Game of Thrones episode was illegally downloaded over a million times in the U.S.).
  • Credit, data & convenience: credit cards trade the privacy of cash for convenience. Who owns the transaction data? Is identity theft the price?
  • Telecommuting: saves time and adds flexibility, but remote workers may be overlooked for promotion.
  • Global networks: the same connectivity lets a company sell worldwide and move jobs abroad.
  • The Web is contested: a channel for democratic ideas, or a tool for totalitarian governments? The technology doesn't decide; people and institutions do.

Foundations of Ethics & Computer Ethics

Ethics"The branch of philosophy that studies principles of right/wrong, good/evil, virtue/vice, and justice/crime."
  • Goal: help people make moral decisions.
  • Assumption: people are rational and free to choose how they act.
  • Asks: What do people think is right? How should people act? What rules or laws should we have?
Computer ethics (James H. Moor)"The analysis of the nature and social impact of computer technology, and the corresponding formulation and justification of policies for the ethical use of such technology."
In plain terms: it analyzes the values in human actions that computer technology now shapes.
The skeptic's case against ethicsWhy we proceed anyway
There are no universal norms of right and wrong
Ethical debate is disagreeable and pointless
Reason alone can't settle moral questions		 Disagreement on hard cases doesn't erase agreement on clear ones
Every profession (medicine, law) reasons under uncertainty
Not deciding is itself a decision, with real consequences

Why computer ethics needed its own field

  1. Policy vacuums: computing creates situations with no existing policy; the rules hadn't been written yet.
  2. Transformed issues: old questions (privacy, property, free speech) return in a form the old answers don't fit.
  3. Genuinely novel issues: problems with no real pre-computing equivalent (e.g. algorithmic bias at scale).

Together these justify a separate field, where a responsible professional actively avoids computer abuse and catastrophe, not just trouble.

Ethical dilemmaA situation in which a person must make a decision about appropriate behavior.

Six steps for resolving an ethical dilemma

  1. Obtain the relevant facts
  2. Identify the ethical issues from the facts
  3. Determine who is affected
  4. Identify the alternatives available to the person who must resolve the dilemma
  5. Identify the likely consequence of each alternative
  6. Decide the appropriate action

Key Terms & the Ethical Point of View

SocietyAn association of people organized under a system of rules meant to advance the good of its members over time.
MoralityA society's rules of conduct: what people ought, and ought not, to do in various situations.
EthicsThe rational examination of morality: an evaluation of people's behavior, not just a description of it.
Core valuesValues almost everyone shares: life, happiness, and the ability to accomplish one's goals.
Selfish point of viewEthical point of view
Consider only your own self and your own core valuesRespect other people and their core values too

Why study ethics?

  • Ethics is a way to decide the best thing to do, weighing advantages and disadvantages, respect for autonomy and justice, deliberately rather than by instinct.
  • New technologies bring new problems where "common wisdom" may not exist yet.

What makes a theory "workable"?

  • Ethics is rational, systematic analysis. "Doing ethics" means explaining your conclusions with facts, shared values and logic, not just asserting them.
  • A workable theory produces explanations that could persuade a skeptical yet open-minded audience, not only people who already agree.
  • A workable theory also takes the ethical point of view (Week 4 recap).

Goal of testing theories: an ethically correct resolution that weighs the welfare of everyone affected, not just the decision-maker. The course tests theories using (1) selected principles of ethical conduct, (2) a taxonomy of ethical types, (3) models of personal and organizational development.

Four recurring scenarios used to test theories: Alexis's ghostwritten scholarship essay; an anti-spam blacklist of ISPs; the East Dakota State Police using a new technology; whether to ship a product due next week. For each, ask: who benefited, who was hurt, what else could have been done?

Kantianism: The First Workable Theory

Good willThe desire to do the right thing. Immanuel Kant: the only thing in the world that is good without qualification is a good will. Reason should cultivate it.
1st Formulation of the Categorical Imperative"Act only from moral rules that you can at the same time will to be universal moral laws."
Test: imagine everyone following your rule. If the rule defeats itself, the act is wrong.
2nd Formulation of the Categorical Imperative"Act so that you treat both yourself and other people as ends in themselves, and never only as a means to an end."
Often the easier test. Quick check: reverse roles. If you'd object to it being done to you, the act likely violates the Categorical Imperative.

Applying the 1st formulation: a promise made to break

  1. Proposed rule: "I may make promises with the intention of later breaking them."
  2. Universalize: if everyone did this, promises would become unbelievable, which contradicts the reason he wants his promise trusted.
  3. Verdict: the rule is self-defeating, so the answer is no.
Case: Carla's purchased report
What happened
A single mother working full time, behind in a demanding history course, buys a report and submits it as her own.
1st formulation
Rule: "You may claim credit for work performed by someone else." Universalized, reports would stop being credible evidence of learning, and professors couldn't credit any report. The rule defeats itself, so it is wrong.
2nd formulation
She tries to deceive her professor, treating the professor as a means (to pass the course) rather than an end. Wrong.

Perfect vs. imperfect duties

Perfect dutyImperfect duty
Must be fulfilled without exception (e.g. telling the truth)Must be fulfilled in general, but not in every instance (e.g. helping others)

When they conflict: a perfect duty prevails over an imperfect one. Two perfect duties in conflict have no clean solution (a real limitation, not a fatal flaw).

Utilitarianism: The Principle of Utility

Founded by English philosophers Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.

Principle of Utility (Greatest Happiness Principle)An action is right to the extent that it increases the total happiness of everyone affected, and wrong to the extent that it decreases it.
UtilityThe power to bring happiness or prevent unhappiness.
Happiness / UnhappinessHappiness = benefit, good, pleasure. Unhappiness = cost, harm, pain.

Act Utilitarianism

  1. Look at consequences. Intentions do not matter; only results count. It is a consequentialist theory.
  2. Add up the change. For each person affected, estimate the gain or loss in happiness, then total everything.
  3. Choose the best act. Sum above zero = good; below zero = bad. The right act has the highest sum.

ExtentHow many people are affected?

Memory aid (not from the slides): I D C P F P E. The first four describe the pleasure itself; the last three describe what follows.

Rawls's principles of justice

  1. Everyone gets basic rights and liberties equal to everyone else's.
  2. Social and economic inequalities are allowed only if (a) everyone has a fair chance at those positions, and (b) they most benefit the least-advantaged (the difference principle).

Virtue Ethics

  • A different question: other theories focus on duties, results or rights. Virtue ethics asks "What kind of person should I be?"
  • Aristotle: happiness comes from a life of virtue, reaching your highest potential (flourishing).
  • Moral virtues (e.g. honesty) are character habits built by doing right. A virtue sits between two vices: courage lies between cowardice and rashness.
  • Right action: an act is right if a good person, acting in character, would do the same in that situation.

What each theory teaches

  • Kantianism: every person is equally valuable; respect others as rational beings.

  • Cellular networks: wireless networks that connect mobile phones.

Spam

SpamUnsolicited, bulk email. It is profitable because sending costs almost nothing: about $10 to send an ad to a million addresses, more than 30,000× cheaper than junk mail.

How spam reaches you and how it is stopped

  1. Get addresses: from websites, chat rooms, newsgroups; viruses harvesting PC address books; dictionary attacks; contests.
  2. Send: most spam is sent by bot herders who control huge networks of computers.
  3. Block: spam filters block most spam before it reaches inboxes.

Why spam needs socio-technical solutions

New technology creates new social situations (calculators → feminization of bookkeeping; telephones → blurred work/home boundaries). Spam is another example. It persists because:

  1. Email messages are practically free
  2. Profits increase with the number of messages sent
  3. So there is strong motivation to send more messages
  4. Internet design allows unfair, one-way communications

What Is a Computer Crime?

Computer crimeA crime that involves a computer system, computer network or any other computer-related technology, either as:
  • An object of a crime: the computer, network or its data is the target of the attack.
  • An instrument used to commit a crime: the computer is the tool used to carry out the offence.

Computer crime includes: intrusions of the public switched network; network integrity violations; privacy violations; industrial espionage; pirated computer software; fraud; Internet/email abuse; murder, terrorism and hacking using computers.

Computer virusA self-propagating computer program designed to alter or destroy a computer system's resources. It attaches itself to software, grows, reproduces many times and spreads, attacking major resources (data, sometimes hardware) until the system is brought down.
HackingA computer attack technique that uses the internetworking between computers and communication devices.
Key idea: as long as computers are not interconnected in a network, hacking cannot take place.

The Four IP Protections (Week 6 version)

1 · Copyright

CopyrightA right, internationally enforceable by law, granted to the creator of an expression. Every original work that has a tangible form and is fixed in a medium is protectable. Duration is set by law; when it expires, the work enters the public domain.

Five rights of the copyright owner: (1) reproduction, (2) distribution, (3) public display, (4) public performance, (5) production of derivative works (e.g. translations, films based on books).

Three criteria for a U.S. copyright:

  1. Fixation: the work has a tangible form (e.g. programs fixed in binary code).
  2. Expression: the work is an expression, not an idea.
  3. Originality: the work is original. Facts and ideas are common property; only their original expression is protected.

2 · Patent

Protects inventions or discoveries: a new and useful process, manufacture or machine (or improvement). Patent law protects the process of carrying out an idea, not the idea itself. Duration e.g. 20 years. Four conditions:

ConditionMeaning
UtilityServes a basic, minimum useful purpose to the public
NoveltyMust be new: not known or published before
Non-obviousnessMust not have been obvious to anyone with ordinary skills
DisclosureAdequate disclosure of the product

3 · Trade secret

Information that gives a business a competitive advantage (formula, design process, device, trade figures). Protection depends on the country, and owners must take active measures to keep it secret. Five characteristics used to judge a trade secret:

  1. Extent to which the information is known outside the business
  2. Extent of measures taken to guard the secrecy
  3. Value of the information to the owner and competitors
  4. Effort or cost by the owner to develop or gather it
  5. Ease or difficulty of acquiring the information

4 · Trademark

A product- or service-identifying label (word, logo, sound) that distinguishes it in consumers' minds. The owner can prevent others from using the same or similar symbol.

CategoryPurpose

Certification markVerifies or authenticates characteristics of a product, service or group of people
Collective markIndicates membership in an organization or association (slide examples: PhD, CPA, MD)

Protecting Software under IP Law; Free & Open Source

Why software is hard to classify: algorithms and ideas are not IP; software is a manifestation of an algorithm, not the algorithm itself; and it's unclear whether software is a product, a service or both. Open questions: should software be copyrighted or patented, where to draw the line, and how to protect legal fair use.

Digital technology makes copying easy: perfect CD/DVD copies, easy downloads, P2P sharing among strangers, compression makes large files feasible, editing tools create derivative works, and scanners convert print to electronic form.

Software under…Key points
CopyrightSoftware and its documentation can be protected: source code, object code, executable code, manuals. Trade-off: registration may require submitting all or part of the software for public inspection, so the developer cannot keep it as a trade secret.
PatentHardware is patentable; software only in certain circumstances, and the issue is not settled. Reasons for debate: total disclosure required; most programs are simple one-person ventures; hard to prove algorithms are processes; cost too high; copyright already gives enough protection. U.S. Supreme Court: software alone could not be patented, but a machine that included software could.
Examples of software patents: data compression (GIF, JPEG, MP3, RSA), Internet tools (pay-per-click ad, browser plug-in), user interfaces (progress bar, force feedback in a game controller).
TrademarkDevelopers embed their marks, which display automatically. There are no effective global trademark laws for programs.
Trade secretOnce flowcharts/blueprints are known, a program is easy to build, so design documents must stay within design circles. Secrets can still spread when employees leave.

IP Foundations: Locke and the Limits of Ownership

Technology changed the game: we benefit from access to high-quality shows, music, movies and programs; the value of IP must be greater than the cost of the media; illegal copying is unavoidable and the Internet spreads copies fast.

Intellectual propertyAny unique product of the human intellect that has commercial value. IP ≠ its physical manifestation: the play is not the paper it is printed on.
John Locke's property rights (The Second Treatise of Government)People have a right to: (1) property in their own person; (2) their own labor; (3) things they remove from Nature through their labor.
As long as: nobody claims more than they can use, and plenty is left over after removal from the common state.

The analogy: writing a play is like making a belt buckle. Mine ore, smelt it and cast it ↔ "mine" words, "smelt" them into prose, "cast" them into a play.

Where the analogy breaks down (these paradoxes weaken the case for a natural right to IP):

  • Two authors, one play: if Ben Jonson and Shakespeare write Hamlet simultaneously, who owns it?
  • Stealing leaves the original: if Ben "steals" the play, both still have it. A stolen belt buckle leaves its owner with nothing.

Protections for Software: Copyrights & Patents

Software copyrights

  • Copyright protection for software began in 1964.
  • Protects the expression of an idea, not the idea itself, and the object program, not the source program. Companies treat source code as a trade secret.
  • Violations: copying a program to give or sell; preloading a program onto a computer being sold; distributing a program over the Internet.
Court caseWhat it established
Apple Computer v. Franklin ComputerObject programs are copyrightable
Sega v. Accolade (slide spells it "Accolate")Disassembling object code to determine technical specifications is fair use
Safe software development: the "clean room" strategyReverse engineering is OK, but companies must protect against unconscious copying.
  1. Team 1 analyzes the competitor's program and writes a specification.
  2. Team 2 uses only the specification to develop the software.
(Compare Arjun's "clean room" option, Week 7.)

Software patents

  • Until 1981 the Patent Office refused software patents, seeing programs as mathematical algorithms, not processes or machines. A U.S. Supreme Court decision led to the first software patent in 1981; later rulings widened what could be patented.
  • Thousands now exist (Microsoft files about 3,000 applications a year); licensing patents is a revenue source.
  • Patent trolls: companies that specialize in buying patents and enforcing patent rights. Most targets settle rather than go to trial; RIM didn't settle quickly and paid $612 million.
  • Criticisms: too many patents issued; the Patent Office doesn't know the prior art, so it issues bad patents; obvious inventions get patents; companies stockpile patents defensively; patents are used as legal weapons.

Legitimacy of IP Protection for Software

Software licenses are legal agreements that usually forbid copying software to sell or give away. The question here is not whether breaking the law is moral, but whether society should give IP protection to software.

ArgumentClaimCriticism
Rights-based: "Just deserts"Programming is hard work only a few can do; programmers should be rewarded for their labor and ought to own their programs.Why does labor imply ownership? One can imagine a just society where all labor goes to the common good. IP is not like physical property.
Utilitarian (consequentialist), chain argument by Beth AndersonCopying reduces software sales → decline in the software industry → fewer new products → fewer benefits to society → copying is wrong.Each link is debatable: not everyone who gets a free copy could afford to buy it; open source shows people will donate their skills; the hardware industry wants to stimulate software; harm to society is hard to quantify.
Conclusion: the natural-rights argument is weak, and the utilitarian argument is not strong either. Nevertheless, society has granted copyright protection to programs, and breaking the law is wrong unless there is a strong overriding moral obligation or consequence.

Open-Source Software

Criticisms of proprietary software

  • Increasingly harsh measures to enforce copyrights
  • Copyrights are not serving their purpose of promoting progress
  • It is wrong to let someone "own" a piece of IP
Open-Source Definition (5 points)
  1. No restrictions preventing others from selling or giving away the software
  2. Source code included in the distribution
  3. No restrictions preventing others from modifying the source code
  4. No restrictions on how people use the software
  5. The same rights apply to everyone receiving redistributions (copyleft)

Week 6 lists the first four; Week 8 adds copyleft.

Beneficial consequences

  • Gives everyone the opportunity to improve a program
  • New versions appear more frequently
  • Eliminates the tension between obeying the law and helping others
  • Programs belong to the entire community
  • Shifts focus from manufacturing to service

Examples: BIND, Apache, Sendmail, Android, Firefox and Chrome, OpenOffice.org, Perl, Python, Ruby, TCL/TK, PHP, Zope, GNU compilers (C, C++, Objective-C, Fortran, Java, Ada), Moodle.

GNU ProjectLinux
Begun by Richard Stallman in 1984; goal: an open-source, Unix-like operating system; most components built in the late 1980s Linus Torvalds wrote a Unix-like kernel in 1991; combined with GNU components to make an OS, commonly called Linux. An alternative to proprietary Unix; ran on 97% of the world's 500 fastest supercomputers (June 2014).

Creative Commons

  • Under current law, eligible works are copyrighted the moment they are created. No copyright notice does not mean it's OK to copy; you must contact the creator first, which slows creative reuse.
  • A free Creative Commons license states which kinds of copying are OK and which rights are retained.
  • Well-known sites using CC licenses: Flickr and Magnatune.

Example shown (Attribution-Noncommercial-Share Alike 3.0): you are free to Share (copy, distribute, transmit) and Remix (adapt), under these conditions: Attribution (credit the author as specified), Noncommercial (no commercial use), Share Alike (derivative works must use the same or a similar license).

7 · Processes, Steps & Key Facts

  • Act utilitarian calculation: consequences only → sum gains/losses for everyone → highest positive sum wins.
  • Kant CI-1 test: state your rule → universalize → self-defeating = wrong.
  • Spam pipeline: get addresses (websites, chat rooms, viruses, dictionary attacks, contests) → send via bot herders → spam filters block.
  • DDoS chain: exhaust resources → reduced function → connectivity broken.
  • Clean room (Week 8): Team 1 analyzes the competitor and writes a spec → Team 2 builds from the spec only.
  • BitTorrent: file split into pieces, each piece downloaded from a different computer.
  • Anderson's chain: copying → fewer sales → industry decline → fewer products → fewer benefits → copying wrong.
  • Key numbers: spam: $10 per million emails, 30,000× cheaper than junk mail · credit card liability limit $50 · identity theft: 1 in 700 cases leads to arrest · software copyright since 1964 · first software patent 1981 · Linux on 97% of top-500 supercomputers (2014) · copyright industries ≈ 6% of U.S. GDP.

8 · Commonly Confused Points

  • Divine Command is "not workable for our purposes"; the other three non-workable theories are simply "not workable".
  • Act utilitarianism says breaking the law is sometimes right; rule utilitarianism and Kant say no; social contract says usually no (only for a higher moral duty).
  • Ethical theories can disagree on the same case: Ann (act util. says good, rule util. says wrong) and Arjun (act util. prefers the riskier option).
  • Kant and Mill both opposed censorship, for different reasons (use your own reason vs. truth from a clash of ideas).
  • The Principle of Harm protects others; protecting someone from themselves is not enough to justify intervention.
  • Self-censorship (not direct censorship) is the most common form.
  • CIPA was upheld 6–3; Pacifica and Sony were 5–4.
  • DDoS does not alter or destroy resources; penetration does.
  • Hacking needs a network; a virus spreads by attaching to software.
  • Patents protect the process of carrying out an idea; copyright protects the expression. Neither protects the idea itself. Algorithms and ideas are not IP.
  • Reverse engineering is allowed for trade secrets and software copyrights, but not for software patents.
  • A trademark can be lost if the brand name becomes a common noun.
  • Free software = freedom, not price.
  • Fair use factors are weighed together. Google Books copied the entire work and still won.
  • DMCA bans circumventing copy controls even for fair use, which is why critics say DRM undermines fair use.
  • Grokster: the lower courts used Sony as precedent; the Supreme Court said the right precedent was Gershwin v. Columbia Artists.
  • Software copyright source vs. object code: Week 6 lists source, object and executable code; Week 8 says object program, not source (source kept as a trade secret). See the flag in Week 6.
  • Strict liability applies regardless of fault; negligence requires a failure of reasonable care.
