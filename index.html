Case Study: Ann the Acme Accountant

Situation: Ann is an accountant at Acme Corporation (American Company Makes Everything). Her job gives her the email list of all 50 employees (she distributes paychecks). She emails everyone advertising a Girl Scout cookie sale (for her daughter).

9recipients order cookies (average 4 boxes each)
40other recipients are unhappy to get the email
20of them (half) complain to a co-worker

Ethical issue: Did Ann do anything wrong by using company email to solicit?

Act utilitarian calculation

Benefit to Girl Scouts = $108: $3 profit per box × 36 boxes (9 buyers × 4 boxes).

Harm to company = $70: time wasted; orders taken during breaks; lost productivity from complaining = 20 employees × 2 × 5 minutes = 200 minutes → 3.5 hours × $20/hour = $70.

* The textbook rounds 200 minutes (≈ 3.3 hours) up to 3.5 hours.

Five theories, five analyses

TheoryReasoningConclusion
Kantian2nd formulation of the Categorical Imperative: treat others as ends in themselves, never only as a means. Ann didn't misrepresent what she was doing, didn't force anyone to read the entire email, and some readers chose to order. But an "opt in" approach would have been better.Not strictly wrong
Act utilitarianBenefits $108 > harms $70, so the action is good. But the company may create a policy against future fundraisers.Good

Ethical perspectives: Kant and Mill

Kant opposed censorship

  • An Enlightenment thinker
  • "Have courage to use your own reason"

Mill opposed censorship

  • No one is infallible
  • Any opinion may contain a kernel of truth
  • Truth is revealed in a clash of ideas
  • Ideas resulting from discourse are more influential
Mill's Principle of Harm
"The only ground on which intervention is justified is to prevent harm to others; the individual's own good is not a sufficient condition."
We may limit someone's freedom to stop them harming others, not just to protect them from themselves. Test: does blocking this content prevent harm to others, or only protect the user's own good?

3.6 Freedom of Expression

A short history

  1. 1275De Scandalis Magnatum (England)
  2. LaterCourt of Star Chamber
  3. 18th centuryNo prior restraints on publication, but people could still be punished for sedition or libel
  4. U.S. statesAmerican states adopt bills of rights, including freedom of expression
  5. 1st AmendmentFreedom of expression in the 1st Amendment of the U.S. Constitution

The 1st Amendment to the U.S. Constitution

"Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the government for a redress of grievances."

In simple words, it protects five freedoms: ReligionSpeechThe pressPeaceful assemblyPetition the government

Freedom of expression is not an absolute right

Freedom of expression
balanced against
The public good
  • The 1st Amendment covers political and nonpolitical speech.
  • The right must be balanced against the public good.
  • Various restrictions on freedom of expression exist.

Two court-tested examples of where the line is drawn

Jeremy Jaynes and the anti-spam law

Convicted: under a Virginia law for sending millions of spam messages.
Overturned: the Supreme Court of Virginia overturned the conviction.
Why? The law was too broad: it also prohibited anonymous transmission of unsolicited bulk emails containing political, religious or other speech protected by the 1st Amendment.
Lesson: a law aimed at something most people dislike can still violate free expression if it is too broad.

FCC v. Pacifica Foundation

1George Carlin records "Filthy Words"
2WBAI in New York airs it (1973)
3FCC issues a declaratory order to Pacifica
4Pacifica sues
5Supreme Court (5–4): FCC did not violate the 1st Amendment

Why broadcasting is different: broadcast media are "uniquely pervasive"; broadcasting is uniquely accessible to children.

Case Study: Kate's Blog

Party gagAt a private birthday party, Jerry (active in the Whig Party) is given a Tory Party T-shirt as a gag and puts it on.
Photo takenKate, who keeps a popular "life on campus" blog, takes his picture with her cell phone when he isn't looking.
PostedShe posts it on her blog. Many people on and off campus read the story.
ConfrontationJerry demands she remove it. She complies and they remain friends.
ResultKate's blog and Jerry both become more popular.

Ethical issue: Did Kate do anything wrong (posting a photo of a friend without permission)?

TheoryReasoningConclusion
KantianShe uploaded Jerry's photo without asking permission; she treated him as a means to her end of increasing her blog's readership.Wrong
Social contractThe party was held in the apartment of one of Jerry's friends, so Jerry had a reasonable expectation of privacy; Kate violated his right to privacy.Wrong
Act utilitarian(+) Kate's blog more popular (definitely); (+) Jerry more popular on campus (definitely); (–) Jerry's anger (only temporary); (–) photo could discredit Jerry later (unlikely). Benefits > harms.Good
Rule utilitarianWhat if everyone constantly took and posted photos of people they met? (+) more chances to keep up with friends; (+) people might avoid illegal activities; (–) people become more self-conscious; (–) some relationships harmed. Negatives are weightier.Bad

Compare the two cases: Ann: theories split (2 say OK, 1 "not strictly wrong", 2 say wrong/fell short). Kate: 4 of 5 say wrong. In both, act utilitarianism approves and rule utilitarianism disapproves; consent / opt-in is the recommended fix.

Key Takeaways

  1. New technology, new social situations: spam needs socio-technical solutions, not just filters.
  2. Who controls the flow? Direct censorship, self-censorship, and Mill's Principle of Harm.
  3. Rights have limits; consent helps: freedom of expression is not absolute. Opt-in and permission settle many cases.

Part 1 · What Is a Computer Crime?

Computer crime
A crime that involves a computer system, computer networks, or any other computer-related technology, either as an object of a crime or an instrument used to commit a crime.

Computer crime includes

  • Intrusions of the public switched network
  • Network integrity violations
  • Privacy violations
  • Industrial espionage
  • Pirated computer software
  • Fraud
  • Internet / email abuse
  • Murder, terrorism and hacking using computers

Core Threats: Virus and Hacking

Computer virus
A self-propagating computer program designed to alter or destroy a computer system's resources.
It attaches itself to software, grows, reproduces many times and spreads in the new environment; it attacks major system resources (data, sometimes hardware) until the system is brought down.
Hacking
A computer attack technique that utilizes the internetworking between computers and communication devices.
Key idea: As long as computers are not interconnected in a network, hacking cannot take place.

Types of Attacks on Computer Systems

T1 · Computer penetration

Computer penetration
Breaking into a system using known security vulnerabilities to gain access to a cyberspace resource.

With full penetration the intruder can

  • Alter data files and change data
  • Plant viruses
  • Install damaging Trojan horse programs
  • Use the victim as a launching pad to attack other network resources

Sources of penetration attacks

  • Insider threat
  • Hackers
  • Criminal groups
  • Hacktivism

T2 · Denial of service (DDoS)

Denial of service attacks, commonly known as distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks, are directed at computers connected to the Internet.

1AimExhaust the network bandwidth, the router processing capacity or network stack resources
2EffectThe system's ability to function is diminished
3ResultNetwork connectivity to the victims is eventually broken
A system can be brought down without destroying its resources: DDoS attacks do not change, alter, destroy or modify system resources.

Penetration versus denial of service

Part 2 · What Is Intellectual Property?

Intellectual property (IP)
Any unique product of the human intellect that has commercial value.
IP ≠ physical property. Examples: books, songs, movies, paintings, drawings, inventions, chemical formulas, computer programs, software.
Intellectual property rights (IPR)
Legal rights given to an individual or a group, in the form of patents, trademarks and copyright, to protect the moral and economic rights of creators.

Copyright holders have exclusive rights to: make copies and produce derivative works (translations, movies based on books); distribute copies; perform or display a work in public.

Two competing goals

Benefit society
We want inventions to reach the public domain without delay.
vs
Reward the creators
We want to promote future inventions.
The solution: governments grant limited ownership rights to IP creators.

Copyright law spreads worldwide

  1. 1710England: copyright laws start
  2. 1790USA: copyright laws start
  3. 1793France: copyright laws start
  4. 1952UCC: Universal Copyright Convention
  5. 1967WIPO: World Intellectual Property Organization

World bodies administering copyright conventions: WIPO · UNESCO · WTO. WIPO and UNESCO are assigned to administer the UCC. Each country still has its own requirements for issuing a copyright.

Some of these laws are not universal: each country may have its own regulations.

Copyrights

Copyright
A right, internationally enforceable by law, granted to an inventor or creator of an expression. Every original work that has a tangible form and is fixed in a medium is protectable.

Five rights of the owner: 1 Reproduction2 Distribution3 Public display4 Public performance5 Production of derivative works

Duration is defined by copyright law. When it expires, the work goes into the public domain.

Getting a copyright in the USA: the application is reviewed against three criteria:

CriterionMeaningNote
FixationThe work has a tangible forme.g., programs fixed in binary code
ExpressionThe work is an expression, not an ideaCreative works are expressions
OriginalityThe work is originalDifferentiates expression from facts
Facts and ideas are common property. Only their original expression is protected.

Patents

In the USA an invention or discovery is patentable if it is new and useful, or a new and useful improvement of a process, manufacture or machine, and it meets four conditions:

UtilityServes a basic and minimum useful purpose to the general public
NoveltyMust be new: not known or published before
Non-obviousnessMust not have been obvious to anyone with ordinary skills
DisclosureThere must be adequate disclosure of the product
  • Patents have a duration (e.g., 20 years).
  • Patent law does not protect an idea, but the process of carrying out an idea.

Trade secrets

Trade secret
Information that gives a company or business a competitive advantage over others in the field.
Examples: formula, design process, device, trade figures. Protection depends on the laws of the country; owners must take active measures to keep their trade secrets from being discovered.

Five characteristics of trade secrets:

  1. Extent to which the information is known outside the business
  2. Extent of measures taken to guard the secrecy
  3. Value of the information to the owner and to competitors
  4. Effort or cost by the owner to develop or gather it
  5. Ease or difficulty of acquiring the information

Trademarks

Trademark
A product or service-identifying label: a word, a logo, a sound, etc., that distinguishes a service or a product in the minds of consumers.
Trademark law gives the owner the right to prevent others from using the same or similar symbol to market their products.

Protecting Computer Software Under IP

Why software is hard to classify

  • Not IP: algorithms and ideas are not classified as intellectual property.
  • Not an algorithm: software is a manifestation of an algorithm, not the algorithm itself.
  • Hard to categorize: is software a product, a service or a mixture of both?

IP protection challenges: Should software be copyrighted? Should software be patented? How to draw the line? How to protect the legal fair use right?

Digital technology makes copying easy

  • The Internet has made copyright infringement easier and cheaper
  • Easy to make perfect copies of CDs and DVDs
  • Easy to download books, music and videos
  • Peer-to-peer networks let strangers share music and other data files
  • New compression technologies make copying large files feasible
  • New tools modify graphics, video and audio files to make derivative works
  • Scanners change the media of a copyrighted work (printed text, photos, artwork → electronic form)

Software under copyright, patent, trademark and trade secret law

Copyright

Software, along with its documentation (including technical manuals), can be protected under copyright laws against illegal copying and piracy: source code, object code, executable code, manuals and documentation.

The trade-off: copyright registration may require submitting the whole or parts of the software for public inspection, so the developer cannot keep the software as a trade secret.

Patent

Hardware components are protected under patent laws. Software can be, under certain circumstances; the issue is not settled yet.

Reasons for the debate:

  • Total disclosure of all information about the application is required
  • Most programs on the market are simple one-person ventures
  • Hard to prove that algorithms are processes, not just a manifestation of an idea
  • The cost is too high
  • Traditional copyright has provided sufficient protection

USA Supreme Court: software alone could not be patented, but a machine that included software could.

Software patent examples

  • Data compression: GIF, JPEG, MP3, RSA
  • Internet tools: pay-per-click ad, browser plug-in
  • User interfaces: progress bar, force feedback in a game controller

Trademarks & trade secrets

Trademarks: developers include their symbols and marks within the product, displayed automatically. There are no effective global trademark laws to protect computer programs.

Trade secrets: once flowcharts or blueprints are known, it is easy to develop a program, so early design documents and flowcharts should be confined to the design circles. Secrets can still spread when employees leave the company.

Source note: Week 6 says source, object and executable code can all be protected by copyright. Week 8 (Quinn) says software copyright covers the object program, not the source program, and companies treat source code as a trade secret. Learn each in its own context. Similarly, Week 6's "software alone could not be patented" contrasts with Week 8's history (first software patent in 1981, then wider patents).

An Alternative Model: Free, Open-Source and Proprietary Software

Free softwareOpen sourceProprietary software
An idea and an ethic, supported by a large, loose network of programmers who allow people to copy, use and modify their software. Free means freedom of use, not necessarily lack of cost. Software distributed or made public in source code, readable and modifiable:
  • No restrictions on selling or giving away the software
  • Source code is included in the distribution
  • No restrictions on modifying the source code
  • No restrictions on how people use the software

1 · Foundations: Why Does IP Exist?

Technology changed the game

  • We benefit: access to high-quality TV shows, music, movies and computer programs.
  • Value must beat cost: the value of intellectual property must be greater than the cost of the media.
  • Copies travel fast: illegal copying is unescapable, and the Internet lets copies spread quickly and widely.
Intellectual property
Any unique product of the human intellect that has commercial value.
Books, songs, movies; paintings, drawings; inventions, chemical formulas, computer programs. IP ≠ its physical manifestation: the play is not the paper it is printed on.

John Locke on property rights (The Second Treatise of Government)

People have a right to…

  1. Property in their own person
  2. Their own labor
  3. Things they remove from Nature through their labor

…as long as

  • Nobody claims more property than they can use
  • After removal from the common state, plenty is left over

Is writing a play like making a belt buckle?

Where the analogy breaks down

Two authors, one play

Ben Jonson and William Shakespeare simultaneously write down Hamlet. Who owns it?

Stealing leaves the original

If Ben "steals" the play from Will, both have it. A stolen belt buckle leaves its owner with nothing.

These paradoxes weaken the argument for a natural right to intellectual property.

A deliberate compromise

Benefits of protection

  • Some people are selfless; some are not
  • The allure of wealth can be an incentive for abstract work
  • The authors of the U.S. Constitution recognized the benefits of limited IP protection

Limits on protection

  • Giving creators rights to their inventions motivates creativity
  • Society benefits most when inventions are in the public domain
  • Congress struck a compromise: rights for a limited time

Option A: "Clean room" strategy

  • Provide the team with performance targets

Kantian analysis

  • Clean room: does not violate the agreement. "You can share your expertise with others" can be universalized: morally right.
  • Personally involved: OK if he asks no leading questions. Leading questions disclose information, and breaking a promise is wrong. Can he realistically avoid them?

Social contract analysis

  • Clean room: Arjun helps the team without violating an agreement. OK.
  • Personally involved: OK if he can avoid disclosing information. If he gets impatient and discloses, not OK.

Actions owners of intellectual property in digital form take to protect their rights.

  • Software copyright protection began in 1964.
  • What gets copyrighted? The expression of an idea, not the idea itself; the object program, not the source program.

Violations of software copyrights

  • Preloading a program onto the hard disk of a computer being sold
  • Distributing a program over the Internet

4.8 Legitimacy of Intellectual Property Protection for Software

Do we have the right system in place?

  • Software licenses typically prevent you from making copies of software to sell or give away.
  • Software licenses are legal agreements.
  • The question here is not the morality of breaking the law, but whether society should give IP protection to software.

Rights-based analysis: the "just deserts" argument

"Just deserts" argument

  • Programming is hard work that only a few can do
  • Programmers should be rewarded for their labor
  • They ought to be able to own their programs

Criticism

  • Why does labor imply ownership?
  • We can imagine a just society in which all labor went to the common good
  • Intellectual property is not like physical property

Utilitarian (consequentialist) analysis

Argument against copying

  1. Copying software reduces software purchases…
  2. …leading to less income for software makers…
  3. …leading to lower production of new software…
  4. …leading to fewer benefits to society

Each of these claims can be debated

  • Not all who get free copies can afford to buy software
  • The open-source movement demonstrates many people are willing to donate their software-writing skills
  • The hardware industry wants to stimulate the software industry
  • Difficult to quantify how much society would be harmed if certain software packages were not released

Conclusion

  • Natural rights argument: weak.
  • Utilitarian argument: not strong, either.
  • Nevertheless, society has granted copyright protection to owners of computer programs.
  • Breaking the law is wrong unless there is a strong overriding moral obligation or consequence.

4.9 Open-Source Software

Criticisms of proprietary software

  • Increasingly harsh measures being taken to enforce copyrights
  • Copyrights are not serving their purpose of promoting progress
  • It is wrong to allow someone to "own" a piece of intellectual property

Open-source definition (5 points)

  1. No restrictions preventing others from selling or giving away software
  2. Source code included in distribution
  3. No restrictions preventing others from modifying source code
  4. No restrictions regarding how people can use software
  5. Same rights apply to everyone receiving redistributions of the software (copyleft)
Copyleft
The same rights apply to everyone receiving redistributions of the software.
If you pass the software on, the next person gets the same freedoms you had.

Week 6 listed the first four points; Quinn (Week 8) adds the fifth, copyleft.

Beneficial consequences of open-source software

  • Gives everyone the opportunity to improve the program
  • New versions of programs appear more frequently
  • Eliminates tension between obeying the law and helping others
  • Programs belong to the entire community
  • Shifts focus from manufacturing to service

4.10 Creative Commons

Streamlining creative re-use

  • Under current copyright law, eligible works are copyrighted the moment they are created.
  • No copyright notice does not mean it's okay to copy.
  • You must contact people before using their work; that slows down creative re-use.
  • A free Creative Commons license indicates which kinds of copying are okay and which rights are being retained.
  • Flickr and Magnatune: two well-known sites using Creative Commons licenses.

Key Takeaways (Week 8, both classes)

  1. DRM = encrypt or mark content. The DMCA bans circumventing it even for fair use. SDMI failed, Sony BMG rootkit backfired, FairPlay was dropped (2009), Xbox One backtracked.
  2. P2P: Napster shut down (2001); FastTrack's supernodes are harder to stop; MGM v. Grokster (2005) reversed the lower courts. BitTorrent splits files into pieces from many peers.
  3. Enforcement: Pirate Bay convicted; PRO-IP Act → domain seizures and the Megaupload shutdown (2012). Streaming is the legal alternative.

  6. Open source (5-point definition incl. copyleft), GNU/Linux, and Creative Commons licenses offer alternatives to strict ownership.

2 · Essential Concepts

  • Spam persists because email is practically free → needs socio-technical solutions.
  • Spam path: get addresses (sites, viruses, dictionary attacks, contests) → bot herders send → filters block.
  • Internet makes censorship hard: many-to-many, dynamic connections, huge numbers of sites, crosses borders, can't tell minors from adults.
  • Kant and Mill both opposed censorship. Freedom of expression is not absolute: balanced against the public good.
  • Hacking needs a network: no interconnection, no hacking.
  • Prevention targets 3 entities: the computer, the criminal, the innocent victim.
  • IP law balances benefit to society vs reward for creators → limited ownership rights.
  • Locke's labor theory is weakened for IP by the Hamlet paradoxes (simultaneous creation; theft leaves the original).
  • Copyright protects expression, not ideas or facts; patents protect the process, not the idea.
  • DMCA: illegal to circumvent encryption/copy controls even for fair use.
  • Software failure causes: human factors + nature of software. Buyer routes: contract or tort.
  • Rights-based and utilitarian arguments for software IP are both weak, but breaking the law is wrong without a strong overriding reason.
